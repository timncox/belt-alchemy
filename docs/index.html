Beltfor Alchemy Lab

Sing into a Eurorack case. Come out in tune, in four parts.

Heard, on one module

Belt has run on a racked Alchemy Lab (22 September 2026): audio in and out, the J3 gate, the SD card picker handing the module to Mark and Smack and back. The number that decided this port, how much of the processor seven pitch-shifting voices take, is now measured. The first build overran; with faster float maths on the engine and its buffers moved into internal RAM, the worst average load was 63.7%, with no overload light and no clicks.

Still untested on the hardware: the CV inputs J4 to J8, a cold power-cycle coming back with its settings, and Belt's own settings surviving a switch to another firmware and back. At the module's 48 kHz a 440 Hz sine still reads as A4 to a hundredth of a semitone in the native test. Claims below about how it sounds with all four voices are still mostly claims about the same engine on the Ableton Move, where it has been sung through.

12 HP · 6 pots · 3 buttons · 10 jacks · 102 LEDs · drawn at true scale

What it does

01 — The idea

A voice goes in on J1. What comes out on J9 and J10 is that voice corrected to a key and scale, at a speed from transparent to robot, with up to four harmony voices that walk the scale from whatever note you sing, a stereo doubler, and a formant control that changes the size of the singer without changing the pitch.

One analysis

Seven voices from one listen

The engine finds your pitch once per 5 ms and lays down a train of pitch marks. Every output voice reads the same marks and re-fires them at its own spacing, so four harmonies and a doubler cost little more than one.

The KEY ring

A tuner in the panel

The ring around the KEY pot shows the scale's notes as dim dots and the note you are singing as a bright one: green within a quarter tone of a scale note, orange outside it. Thirteen LEDs beat four.

Two buttons

HARD, and the harmonies

Hold B1 and correction goes instant and total, the robot. Tap it and that latches. B2 does the same for muting the harmony voices. A gate on J3 punches HARD from a sequencer.

It is the same C engine as Belt for the Ableton Move, recompiled for the Alchemy Lab: six pots on two pages, three settings knobs, three buttons, six CV jacks that read independently of the pots, and 102 LEDs. Fixed latency of 24 milliseconds, with the dry path delayed to match so the wet and dry stay in phase. It also brings firmware swapping from the SD card, so this module can carry several firmwares and change between them from a settings page.

Singing through it

03 — Five steps to a choir
1  Bring a voice up to line level and patch it to J1. J2 too if you have it;
   the engine listens to the two summed and keeps the dry path stereo.
2  Set KEY and SCALE. The ring around KEY lights the scale's notes;
   sing, and your note appears on it, green when you are on one.
3  RETUNE is the speed. 0 is the robot. Start around a quarter of the way up.
4  Hold B3. P1 and P2 are the first two voices' intervals:
   a third and a fifth above out of the box. Let go.
5  Bring up HARMONY. Sing a scale.

Hold B1 for HARD: correction instant and total, whatever RETUNE and AMOUNT say, for as long as you hold. Tap B1 to latch it; tap again to release. The B1 light goes white while HARD is on and is the tuner's colour otherwise. B2 works the same way for the harmonies: hold to mute them, tap to latch the mute. Both buttons stand down while B3 is held, so reaching for Settings never fires either.

FORMANT resizes the singer without moving the pitch: left is bigger, right is smaller, the centre is you. Half an octave each way. AMOUNT is how far towards the scale note the correction goes; at 100 it lands on it. The three engine parameters that did not fit the panel, FLEX, HUMANIZE and WET, are knobs in the Settings pages, and they are the ones to reach for if it sounds too corrected: FLEX leaves singing that is far from any scale note alone, HUMANIZE keeps your vibrato through the correction and gives each harmony voice a slow wander of its own.

What it needs from the signal

A single voice, at line level, reasonably clean. The pitch finder tracks one fundamental between about 92 Hz and 1 kHz, low F2 to high B5, and gates on level: below about −56 dBFS the voices duck and the correction holds its last offset, so breaths and room do not get a harmony. A chord in, or a voice with a loud reverb already on it, gives it two pitches to choose from and it will pick one.

The lowest trackable note is a few hertz higher than on the Move, a consequence of running the same analysis window at 48 kHz instead of 44.1; the latency is two milliseconds shorter for the same reason.

What the voices sing

04 — The engine's scale walk, run for real

A harmony voice does not add a fixed interval. It takes your corrected note, finds its degree in the scale, and walks that many degrees up or down the scale, so a third above C in C major is E, and a third above D is F. This is the engine's own arithmetic on the page: pick a key, a scale and four intervals, then drag the note you are singing.

the KEY ring · P1

Chromatic is the exception: with no scale to walk, the intervals become fixed semitones, a third is four of them, a fifth seven. Octaves are always twelve semitones, whatever the scale. Unison is a voice on your own note, which with HUMANIZE up and SPREAD wide is a second singer. The engine's targets carry half of your vibrato; the doubler is a pair of streams ten cents either side of the corrected lead, panned hard.

Each voice also has a static detune of a few cents and a slow random wander, both scaled by HUMANIZE, so four voices do not sound like one singer copied. With HUMANIZE at zero they lock to the grid and sound like a keyboard, which is sometimes what you want.

Opening the page to look cannot change anything. The SDK's pot catch runs on the way in and on the way out: a SETUP pot only takes effect once you turn it through the value it already holds, and letting go of B3 hands the pots back to the PLAY page without jumping anything. Both pages, SETUP and PLAY, are saved to flash five seconds after you stop moving them, with hands off the buttons, and come back at the next power-up.

At boot the PLAY page keeps its saved values, and the pots catch them. The six pots are shared by every firmware on the card, so after a switch in the picker they point wherever the last firmware left them; adopting them would overwrite Belt's settings with Mark's or Smack's. So a pot does nothing until you turn it through the value Belt saved, then takes over. Only the first boot after flashing, with nothing saved, adopts the pots as they sit.

First boot after flashing seeds the SETUP page with a third and a fifth above, the other two voices off, no doubler, spread 70%.

On module 2 the Settings chord opens, but more slowly with audio patched than in Mark; the likely reason, not yet measured, is the seven voices leaving the control loop less time.

What it remembers

Its own preset slot, so switching firmware loses nothing

Belt keeps its working state, both pages, the Settings knobs and the last session's CPU readings, in preset slot 14, counting from zero (15 in HostLink's list). Each firmware in the family has a slot of its own: Smack 12, Mark 13, Belt 14, Relay 15. Picking Mark in the picker and coming back later finds Belt as you left it. Slots 0 to 11 are for presets saved by hand; saving one by hand into 12 to 15 overwrites that firmware's working state.

Builds before this one autosaved into slot 0. At boot Belt still reads slot 0 once, if it holds Belt's own settings (the schema guard refuses any other firmware's), so a module carries its settings over; once slot 14 holds anything, it wins. Switching away and back has been confirmed on the hardware in Mark and Smack; Belt's own round trip, and a cold power-cycle, are next on the test list.

The boot report

The number this port was waiting on

Seven pitch-shifting voices on a 480 MHz Cortex-M7 was the open question. You cannot watch a status light while singing, so the module measures its own worst-case load and shows it on the P1 ring for the first two and a half seconds of the next power-up.

no data
first boot after flashing

A single dim blue LED means it has not measured anything yet. Green is under 75%, amber under 90%, red means it did not fit.

What the rack said. The first build on module 2 recorded a 100% worst block, and the control loop starved: the Settings chord stopped opening reliably. Two of the three known fallbacks fixed it. The engine is now compiled with faster float maths (its own file only; the SDK and libDaisy keep strict arithmetic), and its buffers moved from the external SDRAM into 160 KB of internal D2 SRAM, because seven voices reading the pitch-mark rings at scattered offsets every sample were waiting on SDRAM. The worst average load since is 63.7%, with no red on B2 and no clicks. The third fallback, fewer voices on by default, has not been needed. The worst single block, what this ring shows, has not been written down since the fix; the average is read over HostLink.

Every knob under CV

07 — J4 to J8, independent of the pots, and a gate on J3

The Alchemy Lab reads pots and jacks separately and the SDK adds them in software, per page and per pot. Five jacks modulate the PLAY page; the sixth is a gate.

JackDrivesPage
J3HARD gate — high is HARD, as if B1 were held—
J4KEY, twelve zones across the CV range; not volt per octavePLAY P1
J5RETUNEPLAY P3
J6AMOUNTPLAY P4
J7HARMONY levelPLAY P5
J8FORMANTPLAY P6

A slow LFO into J8 is the obvious first patch: the singer's size breathing while the pitch stays put. A sequencer into J3 makes HARD rhythmic. J4 steps the key from a sequencer, which changes what every harmony voice sings on the spot.

J3 is read raw in the audio callback, not through the SDK's 20 Hz pot filter, which would round a gate's edges into a swell. It fires on the magnitude of the swing away from the jack's calibrated zero, 1.5 V to assert and 0.5 V to release, so a gate counts whichever way the front end signs it. On module 2 a clock into J3 flashes B1 white in time; at fast rates the light stops matching, because the LEDs redraw at about 60 Hz while J3 is read every 2.7 ms, one 128-sample block. A pulse shorter than a block can fall between two reads, so give it 5 ms or more.

J4 to J8 have not been tried on the hardware yet. The mapping above is what the source sets up.

Volt-per-octave into the harmony target, so a keyboard or sequencer decides what the voices sing instead of the scale, is in Belt's engine on the Move as a branch not yet merged. When it lands there it is the obvious v0.2 here.

Firmware on the card

08 — Carry several firmwares, change between them from the panel

Hermetic's firmware treats the SD card as a place for presets and files. This one also reads .bin files from it, and can write one into the module's firmware slot, verify it, and reboot into it, from a settings page, with the module on and in the rack. Smack and Mark for the Alchemy Lab carry the same page, so any of the three can hand the module to any other.

Prepare the card

FAT32, a folder named alchemy

32 GB or under, MBR partition table, FAT32. Make a folder called alchemy in the root and put firmware images in it: any .bin built for the Alchemy Lab bootloader, this one included. On a Mac, run dot_clean on the card before ejecting so the picker does not list resource forks as files.

Or over USB

Without pulling the card

Files reach the card over USB while the module runs: the web programmer's file panel, or the repository's tools/hostlink-fs.mjs, which makes the folder and puts the images in it from a terminal. Uploads are staged and renamed onto their final name only after the firmware has checked the CRC.

What it refuses, and what it never touches

It checks the image's first eight bytes before erasing anything and refuses one that is not bootable from SRAM. The largest file it will take is the app slot up to the calibration sector, so the board's calibration record and the preset store are never in the erase path. If a write fails, the module blinks red and boots nothing; the bootloader is intact, and hold-B3-at-power-on still gets you a USB flash.

What has run. On module 2 (22 September 2026) the picker switched the module between Belt, Mark and Smack, and picking the firmware already running changed nothing, as above. The card side was proven earlier in Smack: a card in the module written and read back byte-identical over USB, through the mount and buffers the picker uses.

USB and controllers

09 — One front port, three jobs: the Mac, controllers, or a sound card

The front USB-C does one thing per power-up. By default it talks to the Mac: the web programmer, presets, the card. Switched over, the Lab becomes the USB host for a Launchpad Mini MK3, a Launch Control XL and a Haute42 gamepad, or a two-in, two-out USB audio interface.

The USB port setting

Settings page 2, the Firmware page · P6, bottom right

Hold B2 + B3 for two seconds to open Settings, tap B1 to the Firmware page, and turn P6, the bottom-right pot of Settings page 2, the Firmware page, next to the picker. It is a three-way selector:

ModeThe front USB-C is
MacHostLink, the default: the web programmer, presets and the card's files, with the module racked.
LaunchpadThe USB host for controllers. The Lab drives a Launchpad Mini MK3, a Launch Control XL and a Haute42, through a powered dongle; see below. No HostLink this boot.
AudioA class-compliant USB audio interface (USB Audio Class 1): 2 channels in from J1/J2, 2 out to J9/J10, 48 kHz, 24-bit, no driver needed on a Mac. Belt's engine is bypassed: what the computer plays comes out of J9/J10, what comes in on J1/J2 goes to the computer. There is no volume control in the path and J9/J10 are DC-coupled, so a DAW can send CV through them. No HostLink and no controllers this boot.

One setting for the whole card, from the next power-up

The mode is kept on the SD card in /alchemy/usb.cfg, shared by every firmware on it: each one adopts the card's value at boot and writes a change back, so switching firmware in the picker keeps the mode. A change takes effect the next time the module powers up.

Hold B1 while powering up for Mac mode for that one boot, whatever the setting says: the way back to the web programmer without opening Settings. The port has one USB role at a time, so Audio mode means no controllers, and neither Launchpad nor Audio mode has HostLink. The bootloader's DFU (hold B3 at power-on) is unaffected.

Hooking up controllers

A powered USB-C dongle with a hub inside · up to three controllers at once
Power

The front port has no 5 V

The Lab's USB-C gives no bus power, so a controller needs it from somewhere else: a powered USB-C dongle with a USB hub inside. Power the dongle, plug it into the Lab's front USB-C, and plug the controllers into the dongle. The one it was developed with carries a hub and an Ethernet chip; a device that is not a controller, like the Ethernet chip, is switched off and skipped.

Behind the hub

Three at once, one hub deep

Up to three controllers at a time, full speed. Free ports are rescanned every half second, so a controller can go in after power-up. Unplugging one mid-session is not handled tidily: the host sees errors and may reset, so power down to rearrange. The gamepad is only recognised behind a hub, never plugged straight in.

Button positions

The positions here follow the standard GP2040-CE hitbox mapping. If the controller's button mapping has been changed, the same effects follow the XInput button names (X, Y, RB, LB, A, B, RT, LT, the directions, L3, R3), not the physical positions. Start, Back and Guide do nothing.

Download it

10 — The firmware file, or the source

For the Alchemy Lab V2 only. This is a beta: it has been heard on one module, and the CV inputs and a cold boot are still to be tried.

Download belt_alchemy.bin  All releases and notes

  • Install it through the front USB-C as below. To switch firmware from the card, also get Mark and Smack, and put all three .bin files in /alchemy on the microSD card.
  • Belt keeps its working state in preset slot 14. If you saved something in that slot with another firmware, it is overwritten the first time Belt saves. Move it to another slot first.
  • Custom firmware: Hermetic Modular did not write or test it, so please report problems here with an issue, not to them.

To build it yourself instead (a few minutes with the ARM toolchain installed):

git clone --recurse-submodules 
cd belt-alchemy
make libdaisy        # once
make                 # build/belt_alchemy.bin, ~341 KB
make test            # the engine on its allocation pool, and the 48 kHz tuner proof

Needs arm-none-eabi-gcc, make and dfu-util. libDaisy has nested submodules, so the clone must be recursive or the HAL is missing. make BENCH_USB=1 BUILD_DIR=build-bench builds a variant with HostLink on the Daisy Seed's own micro-USB, for a module out of its case on a bench bootloader; it is not the one to rack.

Installing

11 — Through the front USB-C, and getting back to stock

The Alchemy Lab's bootloader serves DFU on the front-panel USB-C, so the module stays in the rack. Once this firmware is on it, so does every update after.

First time

Hold B3 at power-on

During the two-second boot window the rings run a warm-white comet. Hold B3 through it and they switch to a slow breathe with the B3 lights on: update mode. Then flash the .bin with Hermetic's web programmer, or from the repository:

make program-dfu
Every time after

Live

With Belt running, the module can be asked to reboot into update mode over HostLink and flashed in the same breath, no button held:

make program-live

Or put the new .bin on the card and use the picker.

Back to stock

Same door

Flashing any Alchemy Lab firmware, Hermetic's own included, the same way returns the module to it. Presets in the slots survive; Belt's working state stays in slot 14, schema-guarded, so another firmware will not read it as its own.

Before you flash it

It is custom firmware. Hermetic Modular did not write it, did not test it and cannot support it, so please do not contact them about it. It has been heard on one module, its author's. If you run it, the things worth reporting are what the boot report reads after a few minutes of singing with all four voices on, whether the tuner pip lands on the note you sang, and whether CV on J4 to J8 does what the table says.

How it is built

12 — The parts worth knowing about

The engine's C file was not touched. Everything below is the layer between it and the Alchemy SDK, and the handful of decisions that shaped that layer.

PieceWhat it is
The engine, at 48 kHzBelt's engine, byte-identical to the Move build's, with one edit in its header: the sample rate, because libDaisy offers no 44.1 kHz. Left at 44.1 it would have read every note a semitone and a half flat and tuned the singer to a scale that much sharp. A native test holds the retarget: 440 Hz reads note 69.00. The engine's file is compiled with -ffast-math -fno-finite-math-only, a build flag and not a source edit; the rest of the firmware keeps strict IEEE arithmetic.
The analysisYIN pitch detection on the input decimated to 24 kHz, every 256 samples, with a tracking mode that keeps the lag search near the last period. A three-point median kills octave blips. One analysis feeds every voice.
The voicesTime-domain pitch-synchronous overlap-add: two-period Hann grains fired at the target spacing into one stereo accumulator. Formant is the grain content resampled, independent of the spacing, so pitch and size are separate controls.
Two pages, one layerThe SDK's Pager makes SETUP a held layer on B3 with pot catch on the way in and out. Nothing on the PLAY page can jump because you looked at the other one. At boot a saved PLAY page is restored and the pots catch it, so a firmware switch never drags Belt's settings to where another firmware left the pots.
Working presetSlot 14 (0-based) is Belt's live state: both pages, the Settings knobs and the CPU readings. Each firmware in the family has its own (Smack 12, Mark 13, Relay 15). It autosaves five seconds after the last change on either page or in Settings, with hands off the buttons and the picker idle, one 4 KB sector per save. Slot 0, where earlier builds saved, is read once at boot to carry old settings over.
The pickerThree Custom slots on a Settings page: a file scanner, a state machine that compares, then erases, writes and verifies one step per frame, and a DFU hand-off. The compare reads the running image through the memory-mapped flash, so picking the firmware already loaded stops there. Shared, by copy, with Smack and Mark.
Engine pool, in D2 SRAMA bump allocator, so the engine's five callocs land in a pool with no engine edits. It started in external SDRAM; the callback overran there, so it moved to 160 KB of internal D2 SRAM (the engine takes 137,552 B), placed after the LED driver's DMA buffer, which must own the first 32 KB.
HostLinkProduct "Belt", both pages, ten jacks, three buttons, a manual and the SD filesystem, over the front USB-C.
Budget~341 KB image (348,812 B). SRAM 71%, DTCM 72%, D2 pool 160 KB, SDRAM 1.2 MB. CPU: worst average 63.7% on module 2.

Found something, or want it to do something it doesn't? Open an issue. A report from someone singing through it is worth more than anything measurable on a laptop.

Specifications

Appendix
Platform

Alchemy Lab V2

Daisy Seed2 DFM: STM32H750 at 480 MHz, 64 MB SDRAM. 12 HP. Hermetic Modular's Alchemy SDK, beta.

Audio

48 kHz · 128 · 24 ms

Stereo in on J1/J2, summed to mono for analysis; stereo out on J9/J10; fixed 24 ms latency, dry path matched.

Range

92 Hz – 1 kHz

The pitch tracker's range, low F2 to high B5. Twelve keys, nine scales.

Voices

1 + 4 + 2

The corrected lead, four harmony voices at scale intervals from an octave below to an octave above, and a stereo doubler.

Controls

6 × 2 · 3 · 6

Six pots on two pages, three Settings knobs, three buttons, five CV inputs and a gate, 102 LEDs.

Storage

16 slots + card

Sixteen preset slots in QSPI flash, slot 14 Belt's own working state; firmware images and files on a FAT32 microSD.
